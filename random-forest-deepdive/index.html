The forest grows.

Trees can't draw spirals. A hundred of them, averaged, can.

Two spirals.

Two hundred points. A blue arm and a red arm, twisting around each other for two and a half turns. The class boundary is the most non-axis-aligned shape we can draw on a plane.

Where would you cut?

A tree can only cut one way.

Decision trees split with axis-aligned cuts — vertical or horizontal lines, never diagonal. To approximate a spiral they have to step.

Drag the depth slider. Below depth 5 the tree is hopelessly underfit; the boundary looks nothing like a spiral. Past depth 8 it fragments into tiny noise pockets, memorizing individual training points. Train accuracy climbs to 100%; test accuracy plateaus near 74%.

The gap between the two — over twenty percentage points wide — is what overfitting looks like.

The same data, jittered, gives a different tree.

Resample the spirals with a fresh seed. Fit a fresh tree. The boundary doesn't shift politely — it flips, often committing to a different arm at the very first split.

Across the five reseeds, more than 45% of the plane gets a different prediction depending on which one you pick. A tree is a high-variance estimator. We want its prediction without its volatility.

Sample with replacement.

Draw 200 points from the 200-point training set, with replacement. About 63% of the originals show up at least once — some twice, some three times. The remaining 37% don't show up at all.

Those leftover points are out of bag. They've never been seen by this tree, so we can score the tree on them honestly. Every tree comes with its own free held-out validation set.

Sixteen trees, sixteen staircases.

Each tree is grown on its own bootstrap of the data. None of them looks like a spiral — they all look like staircases, but with different arms confidently extended in different directions.

None of these trees is the answer. But every one is a vote.

Average the votes.

Take a query point. Run it through all 200 trees. Count the votes for class 1. The fraction is a probability — and across the plane, that fraction traces a soft spiral that no single tree could draw.

The staircases cancel. The spiral emerges.

How many trees is enough?

Add trees one at a time and watch test error fall from above 30% down to about 18%. After thirty trees the curve plateaus — adding more is cheap insurance, not a real gain.

The purple line is out-of-bag error, computed from each tree's own untouched 37%. It runs higher than test here — at this small training size, generalizing back onto the training points is genuinely harder than predicting fresh draws — but it converges in lockstep, and it costs nothing.

Fifty trials. One tree vs. one forest.

For fifty different random seeds, fit a deep tree and a 200-tree forest on a fresh spiral. Plot the test errors. The single tree is everywhere — its column smears across more than ten percentage points of error. The forest stays in a tight band less than half as wide.

The forest isn't perfect: spirals are intrinsically hard for axis-aligned splitters. But it's stable, and it's better on average. That's the trade.
