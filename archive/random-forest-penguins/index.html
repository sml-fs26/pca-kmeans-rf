One tree is a brittle expert.

A decision tree splits the plane with axis-aligned cuts until every training point sits in its own pure region. Watch one grow.

It's never wrong on training data. That's the problem — the slivers chasing single points won't survive on data the tree hasn't seen.

A second tree disagrees.

Resample the training rows with replacement. Regrow the tree. The boundary moves — sometimes a lot. Repeat five times.

The shared signal emerges where the trees agree. The disagreement concentrates near the true class boundary. That disagreement is information.

Where the diversity comes from. Part 1: the bootstrap.

Each tree trains on its own resample of the data — N rows drawn with replacement from the original N. Some rows show up twice or three times. About 37% don't show up at all.

Those rows are out of bag. They've never been seen by this tree, so its prediction on them is honest — held-out validation, for free, with no separate test set.

Part 2: feature randomness.

At every split, the tree only gets to look at a random subset of mtry features. Without this restriction, every tree would always pick the strongest feature first — and they'd all look alike.

The grove.

One hundred trees, each grown from its own bootstrap, each given a random subset of features at every split. Hover any tree to see its decision boundary alone.

No two are quite the same. They're a parliament of weak voters.

More trees, less variance.

Drag B from 1 to 100. Watch test accuracy climb as the predictions of new trees average in. Training accuracy never moves — it's pinned at 1.0 because the trees are deep enough to memorize.

After about 30 trees the curve plateaus. Adding more trees can't push past the floor set by tree correlation.

How a single prediction is made.

A new query point falls onto the plane. Each of the 100 trees follows its own root-to-leaf path and casts a vote. The forest predicts the majority class; the probability is the vote share.

Drag the point. Near a class boundary the votes split — that's how you read uncertainty out of a forest.

Drag B and ρ. As B → ∞ only the term proportional to ρ survives — that's the floor. Lower mtry drops ρ and lowers the floor, but the trees themselves get weaker. The forest lives in this tradeoff.

Which features matter most?

Each split in each tree reduces the impurity of the data flowing through it. Sum these reductions by the splitting feature, average across trees — that's Gini importance.
